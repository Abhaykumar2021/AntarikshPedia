# Apollo 11

## Why it happened

President Kennedy set the target in a speech on 25 May 1961, forty days
after Yuri Gagarin became the first human in orbit: land a man on the Moon
and return him safely before the decade was out. The commitment was made
against the backdrop of the Cold War, where space achievements read as
proof of technological — and by extension ideological — superiority. NASA's
budget peaked at over four percent of the federal budget in the mid-1960s
to make it happen.

## The mission

Apollo 11 launched on 16 July 1969 on a Saturn V from Kennedy Space Center.
After one and a half orbits of Earth, the third stage reignited to send the
spacecraft toward the Moon. Columbia, the command module piloted by Michael
Collins, stayed in lunar orbit while Eagle, the lunar module carrying Neil
Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, descended on 20 July.

Armstrong took manual control during the final approach to fly past a
boulder field with about 30 seconds of landing fuel margin. At 20:17 UTC,
Eagle landed in the Sea of Tranquility. Six and a half hours later,
Armstrong stepped onto the surface, followed by Aldrin. They spent 21.5
hours on the Moon, deployed experiments, and collected 21.5 kg of samples
before rendezvousing with Collins and returning to Earth on 24 July,
splashing down in the Pacific.

## Results

Apollo 11 met Kennedy's deadline with five months to spare. The samples
confirmed a volcanic origin for the mare basalts of the landing site, and
the seismometer left behind transmitted data on moonquakes for three weeks.
Two more precise facts matter for everything after it: 21.5 kg of rock came
home for laboratories on Earth, and rendezvous-and-docking — the technique
every later mission from Skylab to the ISS depends on — was demonstrated
end to end.
